## Current Focus

My current focus is institutional AI strategy: the practical layer between AI
ambition, governance, teaching and research needs, operational readiness, and
working software.

I am especially interested in questions like:

- How should an institution decide which AI uses are appropriate, useful, and
  aligned with its mission?
- How should AI initiatives be governed across research, teaching, learning,
  administration, accessibility, privacy, and operations?
- How can leaders translate responsible AI principles into systems, policies,
  procurement patterns, training, and day-to-day practice?
- How can AI-assisted work preserve provenance, reviewability, consent,
  recoverability, and human judgment?
- How can institutions build capability without turning AI into either a toy,
  a threat, or an unaccountable hidden layer?

## Selected Work Themes

### Local-First AI Memory and Agent Context

One of my current focus areas is durable AI memory: not as a vague chatbot
feature, but as infrastructure with provenance, lifecycle state, human review,
project context, and recovery paths.

This includes work on Memoria, a local-first memory and context system for
AI-assisted development. The project explores:

- append-only raw events;
- traceable derived memories;
- memory lifecycle states;
- context packs for startup, path editing, review, and handoff;
- repository and path context;
- review queues;
- session ledgers;
- SQLite-backed local storage;
- HTTP and MCP access;
- backup and integrity workflows.

The larger question behind the work is institutional:

> If AI systems are going to participate in serious work, what does responsible
> memory look like?

### Higher-Education and Institutional Systems

I have worked deeply in higher-education web and digital systems, including
student-facing platforms, recruitment and enrolment workflows, content
operations, campaign tooling, local development environments, integrations, and
documentation.

This work matters because institutional systems are rarely just "websites."
They are trust surfaces. They shape how people find programs, understand
requirements, complete processes, and decide whether an institution feels
accessible or hostile.

The best institutional software reduces ambiguity. It helps people move
through complexity without feeling punished by the system.

### Accessibility, Ethics, and Human-Centred Systems

Accessibility is not a polish layer. It is a systems requirement.

I care about software that respects bodies, minds, time, attention, fatigue,
language, mobility, administrative burden, and the many invisible constraints
people carry into every interface.

That perspective affects how I think about:

- forms and workflows;
- keyboard and screen-reader behaviour;
- plain-language interfaces;
- error states;
- institutional bureaucracy;
- AI consent and memory;
- documentation;
- maintenance expectations;
- who gets excluded when a system is designed around an imaginary average
  user.

### Compilers, Tools, and Low-Level Systems

I also work below the application layer. My project history includes compiler
work, Rust systems, Go tools, graphics and engine prototypes, networking,
infrastructure, and embedded/controller-adjacent experiments.

This layer matters because it changes how I approach software at every level. I
am comfortable thinking about systems from the UI down to storage, runtime
behaviour, protocols, deployment, and the shape of the abstractions themselves.

### Publishing, Documentation, and Translation

A recurring theme in my work is making complex material legible.

That can mean writing technical documentation, explaining infrastructure to
non-specialists, building admin tools that encode domain rules, or designing
portfolio materials that turn a large private body of work into public-safe
evidence.

Good software is not only code that runs. It is also code that can be
understood, inherited, operated, and trusted.

### Broad Creative and Technical Practice

My technical work sits alongside a wider practice of making, repairing, and
understanding systems in physical space.

That includes marketing and analytics, web development, design, creative
projects, textile craft, woodworking, metal work, electrical engineering,
embedded systems, home infrastructure, security, and networking.

I do not treat those as disconnected hobbies. They exercise the same kind of
attention: materials, constraints, tools, failure modes, ergonomics,
maintenance, and the difference between something that merely works once and
something that can be lived with.

That matters for software. A person who has built, repaired, wired, hosted,
stitched, routed, measured, designed, and maintained real things tends to think
about systems differently. The work becomes less abstract. Interfaces have
weight. Failure has consequences. Maintenance becomes part of the design.

## Why I Build

I build because systems shape people.

A form can exclude someone. A workflow can exhaust someone. A missing audit
trail can make accountability impossible. A fragile platform can quietly become
an institution's real policy. A poorly deployed AI system can remember the wrong
things, forget the right things, and make responsibility harder to locate.

I want the opposite kind of software:

- systems that make context easier to recover;
- tools that reduce unnecessary friction;
- interfaces that respect different bodies and minds;
- infrastructure that can be inspected and repaired;
- AI workflows that preserve consent, provenance, and human judgment;
- software that helps institutions become more humane instead of merely more
  automated.

That is the throughline.
